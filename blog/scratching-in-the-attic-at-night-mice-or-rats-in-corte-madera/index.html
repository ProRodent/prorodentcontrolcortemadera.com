Scratching in the Attic at Night: Mice or Rats?

Not every attic noise means the same animal or the same fix. Here is how to read the clues before calling.

Why the sound alone is not enough.

Scratching, scurrying and light thumping in an attic all sound similar from below a ceiling. The acoustics of a roof cavity can make a small mouse sound larger than it is, or a rat sound like nothing more than a settling house. Rather than guessing from sound alone, a few other clues narrow things down considerably.

Timing and pattern.

Mice tend to be active in short bursts throughout the night, since they make frequent trips between a nest and a food source. Rats, especially roof rats moving along a consistent route from a tree limb to a roofline, often produce a more regular, heavier pattern of movement at specific times, particularly right after dusk when they first become active for the night.

Droppings tell the clearest story.

  • Mouse droppings: about 1/8 to 1/4 inch, pointed at both ends, found in small clusters.
  • Rat droppings: about 1/2 to 3/4 inch, blunt-ended, often found along a consistent travel path.
  • Fresh droppings are dark and moist-looking; old ones are dry and grey.
  • Both species leave more droppings near their nest than along open travel routes.
The droppings tell you more than the sound ever will.

Gnaw marks and entry points.

Rats need a gap of roughly half an inch to squeeze through, about the size of a quarter, while mice manage with a quarter-inch gap, closer to the width of a pencil. If you can find where the sound seems to originate and check nearby vents, roofline gaps or soffit corners for a chewed opening, the size of that opening is one of the more reliable clues available without a professional inspection.

Why getting this right matters for treatment.

Mice and rats respond to different trap sizes, different bait placement, and different sealing materials given the size difference in what they can squeeze through. Our mouse control and rat control services are built around these differences rather than treating every rodent call identically. If you are not sure which one you are dealing with, a rodent inspection and estimates visit settles it definitively.

What to do while you wait for a visit.

Avoid disturbing any droppings you find, since dry droppings can release contaminants into the air if swept or vacuumed. Note the time of night you hear activity and roughly which part of the attic it seems to come from, since that detail helps a technician plan where to check first once they arrive.

Why the attic specifically.

Attics offer a stable, dry, temperature-buffered space that both mice and rats find appealing, especially compared to fluctuating outdoor conditions. Insulation adds warmth and material for nesting, and the relative lack of foot traffic in most homes means an attic can host activity for weeks before anyone notices anything beyond the occasional noise at night. That combination, comfort and low disturbance, is exactly why attics are one of the most common sites for rodent activity across Corte Madera.

When the noise is not rodents at all.

Not every attic noise is a rodent. Birds nesting under eaves, squirrels using a roofline as a highway, or even a loose piece of flashing moving in the wind can all produce sounds that get mistaken for rodent activity. A quick way to narrow it down: rodent activity tends to be more consistent night after night and often comes with droppings or gnaw marks, while a one-off noise during a windy night is more likely something else entirely. If you are unsure, describing the pattern and timing on the phone helps a technician make an educated guess before even arriving.

Regardless of which animal or source turns out to be responsible, avoid the temptation to go poke around the attic yourself at night trying to catch it in the act. Daytime inspection with proper protective equipment is both safer and more effective, since most rodents are far less active during daylight hours and a nighttime search in a cramped attic space carries its own risks beyond the rodent question itself.

Why waiting to see if it stops rarely pays off.

It is tempting to wait a few nights to see if the noise stops on its own, especially if it is not happening every single night. In practice, an established nest tends to persist and grow rather than resolve itself, and the longer it goes unaddressed, the more droppings, gnaw damage and potential contamination accumulate in the meantime. A call placed after the first clear night of activity, rather than after a week of listening and hoping, generally leads to a smaller, less expensive fix.

If the noise turns out to be something other than a rodent, that is still useful information worth having. It rules out a concern and points toward the right specialist, whether that is someone who handles birds and wildlife or simply a roofer checking loose flashing.

Timing patterns worth noting before you call.

Mentioning when the noise happens helps narrow things down before a technician even arrives. Activity concentrated in the couple of hours right after dark, tapering off by midnight, leans toward mouse behavior more often than not. Noise that continues steadily through the night, or that includes a heavier, more deliberate-sounding movement, leans toward rat activity. Neither pattern is a certain diagnosis on its own, but passing that detail along on the phone lets a technician arrive already thinking about the more likely scenario.

Even a rough description over the phone, such as scratching that starts right at dusk versus scratching that seems to happen at random hours, helps a technician arrive prepared rather than starting the diagnostic process from zero.

A technician who hears about the timing pattern beforehand can also bring the right tools for that specific scenario, whether that means a thermal camera for a quiet, hard-to-pinpoint case or standard inspection gear for a more obvious one.

Common questions

Can I tell mice from rats by sound alone?

Not reliably. Attic acoustics distort sound in ways that make size hard to judge, so droppings and gnaw marks are more reliable clues.

Is it dangerous to check the attic myself?

You can look without disturbing anything, but avoid touching or sweeping droppings, and consider a mask if you plan to spend time up there.

What if I find both mouse and rat sign in the same attic?

It happens. Both species can be present at once, especially in an attic with more than one entry point, and a technician treats each according to what is actually found.
