Winter Rains and Burrow Flooding on Marin Hillsides

A wet winter changes where outdoor rats spend their time, and that shift can bring activity closer to a house than usual.

Why heavy rain matters for outdoor rat activity.

Norway rats burrow at ground level, and a burrow low on a hillside or near a drainage path can flood during a heavy winter storm. When that happens, rats look for higher, drier ground quickly, which sometimes means moving closer to a house that sits above the flooded area, especially one with an unsealed foundation gap or crawl space vent.

Why this is a seasonal, not year-round, pattern.

Corte Madera's dry summers mean this kind of displacement is specific to the wet winter months, generally from late fall through early spring depending on that year's rainfall. A property that has never had indoor rat activity can see its first sign during a particularly wet stretch, which sometimes gets mistaken for an unrelated new problem rather than a seasonal shift.

  • Watch for new burrow activity higher up a slope after a heavy rain event.
  • Check foundation vents and crawl space access after any period of sustained rain.
  • Note whether outdoor activity increases specifically during or right after a storm.
  • Consider a check before the rainy season if your property sits below open hillside.
Displaced rats look for the nearest dry ground higher up.

What this means for hillside properties.

Homes below open space near Ring Mountain Nature Preserve are more exposed to this pattern than homes in flatter, more built-up parts of Corte Madera. That does not mean every hillside property will see an issue every winter, but it is a real factor worth planning around if you have had any past activity.

What to do about it.

A pre-winter check through rodent inspection and estimates can confirm whether your property's specific foundation and crawl space gaps are sealed before the rainy season starts. If burrow activity does show up during a wet stretch, backyard and outdoor rodent control addresses it directly rather than waiting for it to move indoors.

How much rain actually triggers this.

There is no single rainfall threshold that reliably predicts burrow flooding, since it depends on soil type, existing drainage, and how saturated the ground already is from prior storms that season. A single heavy storm after a dry stretch may not cause much displacement, while a moderate storm following weeks of already-wet ground can flood burrows that survived earlier, heavier rain. This is part of why an individual property's history matters more than a general seasonal rule.

What long-term Corte Madera residents have observed.

Homeowners who have lived on hillside-adjacent properties for several winters often develop a rough sense of which storms tend to bring new activity and which do not, based on their own property's drainage and past experience. That kind of accumulated observation is genuinely useful and worth mentioning to a technician, since it can help focus an inspection on the specific conditions that have mattered historically at that address rather than a generic hillside checklist.

If you are new to a hillside property and do not yet have that history, treat the first wet winter as a learning period. A bit of extra attention to any new activity is a reasonable way to start building a sense of how your specific property responds to heavy rain.

How our services connect to this pattern.

Backyard and outdoor rodent control treats displaced burrow activity directly during a wet stretch. A pre-winter rodent inspection and estimates visit confirms foundation and crawl space gaps are sealed before the rains test them, and rodent exclusion closes anything found before it becomes the route a displaced rat uses to get indoors.

What to tell a technician about your property's history.

If your property has had rodent activity in past wet winters, mentioning which storms or months tended to trigger it is useful. Noting where on the property activity showed up gives a technician a real head start compared to a first-time call with no history to reference. Even a rough memory, such as it always seems to happen after the first big storm of the season, is useful context worth sharing.

What happens on properties with existing exclusion work.

A property that has already had a thorough rodent exclusion visit is considerably less vulnerable to this seasonal pattern than one that has never been checked. The entry points a displaced rat would use to get from a flooded burrow into the house are already closed. This is one of the clearer examples of how preventive sealing work pays off specifically during a wet winter, even on a property that has never had an indoor rat problem before.

It has likely been more than a year or two since some properties' last exclusion check. If you live below or near open hillside, a check before the rainy season starts is a reasonable way to confirm that earlier work is still holding, rather than assuming a past visit means the property is permanently protected.

How the first big storm of the season differs.

The very first significant storm after a long dry summer tends to produce more displacement activity than storms later in the season, since ground that has gone completely dry for months absorbs water differently than soil that is already saturated from earlier rain. That first storm often catches burrows at their most vulnerable, which is part of why Corte Madera calls related to this pattern tend to cluster right after the season's opening rain rather than spreading evenly across the winter.

Keeping this seasonal pattern in mind, rather than treating every rainy-season sighting as an unrelated new problem, helps put a single fall or winter encounter in the right context and points toward the right next step for your specific property.

Property owners further from open hillside, in the flatter parts of Corte Madera, see this pattern far less often, but a low-lying yard with poor drainage can still produce a milder version of the same effect during an unusually wet stretch.

Common questions

Does every hillside property see this during winter?

Not necessarily every year, but properties below open space are more exposed to this pattern than flatter, more built-up lots.

How do I know if new activity is from burrow flooding or something else?

Timing is the clearest clue. Activity that starts specifically during or right after a heavy rain event points toward displacement rather than an unrelated new problem.

Does the activity go away once the rain stops?

Often, displaced rats return to their original burrows once conditions dry out, though any new entry point they found into a structure in the meantime needs to be sealed regardless.

Is there a way to prevent this ahead of time?

A pre-winter inspection to confirm foundation and crawl space gaps are sealed is the most direct way to reduce the risk before a wet season starts.
