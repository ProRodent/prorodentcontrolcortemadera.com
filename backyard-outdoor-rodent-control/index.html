Where outdoor activity tends to show up.

Yards close to the open hillside below Ring Mountain Nature Preserve see more burrowing and outdoor rat traffic than flatter, more built-up parts of Corte Madera, simply because there is more natural cover nearby. Compost areas, dense hedges, woodpiles and sheds all give rodents shelter close to a food source, and a burrow that starts along a fence line can spread if it goes unaddressed.

Properties close to Menke Park and other older, established landscaping tend to have mature trees and hedges that give both mice and rats cover close to a house, even when the activity has not moved indoors yet.

Common outdoor problem spots.

  • Burrows along fence lines, retaining walls, and the base of sheds.
  • Compost bins and areas where food waste accumulates.
  • Woodpiles and dense ground cover close to the house.
  • Overgrown hedges and vegetation touching exterior walls.
An outdoor burrow rarely stays outdoors for long.

How we address yard activity.

We locate active burrows and travel routes, then place traps and tamper-resistant stations along those paths rather than throughout the whole yard. Where a burrow is confirmed active, we treat it directly rather than simply filling it in, since a rat can dig back out or start a new one nearby if the underlying activity is not addressed first.

Reducing what draws rodents to a yard.

Keeping woodpiles raised on a rack and away from the house, securing compost and trash, and trimming vegetation back from exterior walls all reduce how attractive a yard is to rodents in the first place. None of these steps alone solve an active problem, but they make treatment last longer once the current activity is cleared.

Seasonal patterns worth watching for.

Outdoor rodent activity in Corte Madera tends to pick up in fall as cooler nights push animals to look for steadier food and shelter, and again in late winter after wet weather that floods lower burrows near a hillside edge. A yard that has been quiet all summer can suddenly show fresh burrow activity within a week or two of the first real cold snap. An early call before winter often costs less effort than waiting until droppings and gnaw marks are widespread.

If the yard work uncovers signs that animals have already found a way into the house itself, such as a gap under a garage door or a gnawed vent screen, we flag that during the same visit rather than treating the yard in isolation. Pairing outdoor treatment with rodent trapping and removal indoors is common on properties where both signs show up at once, and keeps the two sides of the problem from being addressed on separate schedules.

Working around pets and children outdoors.

Outdoor placements are chosen with pets and children in mind just as much as indoor ones. Tamper-resistant stations are used along fence lines and near sheds, kept away from areas where a dog regularly digs or a child's play equipment sits. If you have a specific spot in the yard that gets heavy use, mention it when you call so placement can work around it rather than through it.

Sheds and detached storage.

A shed with a dirt floor or a gap along its base is an easy shelter for both mice and rats, especially when it sits close to a fence line or hedge. We check sheds as part of a full yard visit, looking for burrow entrances at the base and gnaw marks on stored items inside, and treat them the same as any other outdoor structure rather than skipping past them.

A yard visit typically ends with a short walk-through of what was found, where treatment was placed, and what to watch for over the following weeks. If the yard borders a neighbor's property with its own rodent activity, we mention that too, since a shared boundary sometimes benefits from coordinated treatment on both sides.

Rock walls and dry-stacked stone borders, common in older Corte Madera landscaping, create natural voids that rats use as ready-made burrows without needing to dig much at all. These features get checked specifically during a yard visit, since a rock wall with active use can look undisturbed from a distance while still hosting a den underneath.

Before we arrive

  • Keep children and pets away from any area with droppings, a nest, or a suspected entry point.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings; that can spread contamination into the air.
  • Do not set out your own loose bait or poison before the visit.
  • Note when and where you have seen or heard activity so the technician can start there.

Questions about backyard and outdoor rodent control

Should I just fill in a burrow I found?

Filling it in without treating the activity first often just leads to it being dug open again or a new burrow starting nearby. Have it checked before filling it.

Are compost bins safe to keep if I have rodent activity?

A secured, raised, secured compost bin away from the house is manageable. An open pile or one right against a wall is a common draw for rodents.

Does trimming hedges really make a difference?

Yes, dense vegetation touching a house gives rodents cover right at the entry points they'd otherwise have to cross open ground to reach.

Can outdoor rodent activity move indoors on its own?

Yes, especially if there is any gap in the foundation, roofline or garage. That's why we recommend checking both together if outdoor activity is heavy.

Do you treat properties bordering Ring Mountain Nature Preserve differently?

The approach follows the same principles, but we account for the extra natural cover nearby when deciding where treatment will have the most effect.
